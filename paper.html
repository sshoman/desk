Agentic Learning Without Retention

From Experience to Transferable Worldviews
Salah Shoman · Independent Researcher · Revised November 2026

Abstract

Agentic systems increasingly retain interaction histories, summaries, and retrieved episodes to support future tasks. However, these records preserve more than useful observations. They can also preserve the original agent's interpretations, assumptions, and conclusions that were never independently tested. When such records are passed to another agent, unsupported beliefs become part of its starting knowledge and shape its thinking patterns. Learning Without Retention (LWR) is a framework that separates learning from episodic storage. LWR treats experience as temporary evidence rather than knowledge to be inherited. Its consolidation process uses failures and other informative discrepancies to generate competing hypotheses, attempts to kill those hypotheses through counterexamples, interventions, and prospective tests, and converts only surviving, conditional explanations into principles. Successful outcomes also contribute evidence by helping establish where a principle holds and where its boundaries lie. Principles are then connected into a versioned worldview: a structured, revisable account of relationships that a new agent can use without receiving the episodes or unsupported assumptions from which it was built. The framework specifies an evidence lifecycle, assumption controls, a refutation process, a principle-to-worldview composition layer, and a source-free handoff. It also proposes experiments to test whether this process reduces assumption carryover and improves decision transfer under matched budgets.

Keywords: agent learning, episodic memory, knowledge consolidation, failure analysis, hypothesis testing, refutation, principle formation, worldview construction, assumption filtering, transfer

1. Introduction

An agent that completes a task can preserve a record of what happened. It can summarize that record, retrieve it later, and use it while facing a new task. This creates continuity, but continuity is not the same as learning. A stored episode can show what an agent did without establishing why the outcome followed. A summary can shorten the episode without improving the reliability of the explanation it contains.

The problem is not limited to incorrect observations. An experience often combines several kinds of content: events that were directly observed, interpretations generated by the agent, assumptions used to connect those events, and details that remain unknown. When these categories are stored or compressed as one object, their boundaries can disappear. A later agent may receive an interpretation as though it were an observation, or inherit an assumption simply because it appeared in a successful sequence.

This creates a specific form of knowledge contamination. An assumption can move from an episode into a summary, from the summary into persistent memory, and from that memory into another agent's decisions without ever being tested. Repetition does not solve the problem. Several records may repeat the same original assumption, while several logs from one incident may appear to provide independent support when they do not. Compression can make an assumption harder to notice while leaving its influence intact.

Learning Without Retention (LWR) separates the material used to learn from the knowledge allowed to persist. Experiences are temporary evidence. A consolidation process decomposes them, identifies informative discrepancies, generates competing hypotheses, and tries to reject those hypotheses with tests that could expose their limits. A hypothesis that survives the relevant tests may be expressed as a conditional principle. Principles are not stored as disconnected tips: they are connected, compared, and revised within a structured worldview. The receiving agent gets that worldview and its evidence certificates, not the source episodes.

Failure has an important place in this process, but it is not the whole process. A failed action, a prediction mismatch, a counterexample, or an explanation that does not transfer can expose a gap between what the agent expected and what the evidence shows. That gap is often a particularly useful starting point because it makes an assumption or boundary visible. Yet a failure is not an explanation. The agent must generate possible explanations and try to kill them. Successful outcomes matter too: they can test predictions, show that a proposed mechanism works under specified conditions, and help identify the boundary between cases where a principle applies and cases where it does not. LWR therefore gives informative failures high diagnostic priority without treating failure as the exclusive source of knowledge.

The learning path can be summarized as:

Failure or other informative discrepancy → competing hypotheses → kill or revise → conditional principle → connected worldview.

The arrow from principle to worldview represents composition rather than a final one-way step. Principles become connected parts of a larger account. That worldview can then guide later expectations, expose contradictions, and generate new hypotheses. Learning is therefore iterative: the worldview shapes future investigation, while new evidence can revise the worldview.

LWR introduces Shaman as a concrete architecture for implementing this process. Shaman is a separate learning layer rather than a prompt that asks the active agent to reflect more carefully. It has its own evidence records, hypothesis versions, test protocol, promotion gates, worldview publication process, and deletion boundary. The active agent can continue working while Shaman consolidates selected events asynchronously.

The paper makes five contributions:

1. It defines LWR as a separation between temporary experience and persistent, transferable understanding.

2. It specifies how failures and other informative discrepancies generate competing hypotheses, while successful outcomes and control cases help define scope and boundaries.

3. It introduces an explicit kill stage in which hypotheses face counterexamples, interventions, prospective predictions, and independent evaluation before any principle is promoted.

4. It defines a principle-to-worldview layer that connects validated principles, tracks their dependencies and conflicts, and preserves uncertainty rather than turning a collection of tips into an apparently complete account.

5. It proposes controlled experiments that measure assumption carryover, false promotion, principle quality, worldview coherence, and cross-agent transfer after source episodes are made unavailable.

The proposal is intentionally testable. It does not claim that LWR already improves agent performance. Its claim is that agent learning should be evaluated by what a new agent can use after the source experiences and their unsupported interpretations are no longer available. The experiments specify how that claim could be supported or rejected.

2. The LWR Learning Principle

2.1 Experience is evidence, not inherited knowledge

An experience is a record of an interaction in a particular setting. It may include the task, the agent's action, its expected outcome, the observed outcome, tool responses, and a narrative written after the event. This record is valuable because it provides material from which a system can learn. It is not automatically a reliable explanation of the event.

LWR separates six objects that ordinary memory pipelines often collapse:

ObjectMeaningAllowed role
ExperienceA bounded record of an event or task episode.Temporary source material for investigation.
EvidenceA sourced observation or verified outcome extracted from an experience.Supports or challenges candidate explanations.
HypothesisA provisional, scoped explanation with predictions, assumptions, alternatives, and possible falsifiers.Enters testing; cannot guide normal retrieval.
Test resultThe independently recorded outcome of a registered test.Determines whether a hypothesis is supported, contradicted, inconclusive, or invalid.
PrincipleA conditional claim that has passed defined evidence and transfer gates.May be connected to the worldview and retrieved within its scope.
WorldviewA versioned structure of principles and their relationships, conditions, conflicts, and dependencies.Guides a new agent while remaining open to revision.

The distinction matters because these objects have different jobs. An experience preserves a local event. Evidence records what can be verified about it. A hypothesis proposes why it happened. A principle describes a relationship expected to hold across a defined range of cases. A worldview organizes principles into a larger account of how parts of a domain relate. A principle is not a shorter retelling of an episode, and a worldview is not a transcript archive.

2.2 Why failure is a high-value learning signal

A failure becomes informative when it reveals a mismatch between an expectation and an observed outcome. The mismatch can expose an overly broad rule, a hidden premise, a mistaken causal story, a missing condition, or a boundary the agent did not know existed. A failed attempt can therefore provide more diagnostic information than an ordinary success. A success may show that one route worked; a failure can reveal that the agent's explanation does not cover the case it was meant to explain.

The value is not in the negative outcome by itself. A failed action caused by a temporary network outage may say little about the agent's strategy. A tool may fail for reasons outside the agent's control. A task may be underspecified. A test may be invalid. LWR therefore records the expected outcome, observed outcome, source reliability, and environment conditions before deciding whether an event contains a useful discrepancy. It does not translate every failed task into a lesson.

Failure can enter the learning process in several forms:

  • Execution failure: an attempted action does not achieve its intended result.
  • Prediction failure: the observed result differs from a prediction made before the outcome was known.
  • Explanation failure: an existing explanation fits earlier cases but fails on a new case or counterexample.
  • Transfer failure: a principle works in one task family but fails when applied outside its tested scope.
  • Near miss: the task succeeds, but only because of a condition, intervention, or recovery the agent did not account for.

These categories should not be treated as interchangeable. An execution failure may be caused by the environment; a prediction failure directly challenges a registered expectation; an explanation failure challenges the scope or mechanism of a claim. Their shared value is that they reveal where the current account may be incomplete.

The system should prioritize events by information value, not by a simple success/failure label. A surprising success can be highly informative when it contradicts an expectation or reveals an unexpected condition. A routine success may be sampled as a control. A cluster of similar failures may be more informative than one dramatic failure, but several logs from the same incident must not be counted as independent evidence.

2.3 Success is evidence too

A failure can expose a weakness in an explanation, but it usually cannot establish the complete replacement explanation on its own. Successful cases help test whether a revised hypothesis predicts the outcomes it claims to explain. They can also identify conditions under which a principle works, distinguish a genuine mechanism from a coincidental association, and show whether a proposed boundary is too narrow.

Success is not automatically reliable evidence. An agent can succeed by luck, through an unobserved external intervention, or despite using an incorrect explanation. One successful trajectory does not prove that every step was necessary. LWR therefore treats success and failure symmetrically as observed outcomes, but not as equally diagnostic in every context. Event selection gives additional attention to unexpected outcomes and prediction mismatches because they often carry more information than routine repetitions.

A useful comparison is between two questions. A failure asks, "What expectation or assumption did this outcome challenge?" A success asks, "Which parts of the proposed explanation were actually needed, and under what conditions did they work?" Both questions are necessary to form a principle with a meaningful scope.

2.4 A mathematical example: from a false rule to a principle

Mathematics makes the role of failure especially clear because a counterexample can decisively disprove a universal claim. Consider an agent that proposes the identity:

(a + b)^2 = a^2 + b^2

The rule may look plausible if the agent has noticed that squaring distributes over some individual terms in other contexts. But the universal claim can be killed with a simple counterexample. Let a = 1 and b = 1. The left side is 4; the right side is 2. The hypothesis is false.

The counterexample does not, by itself, supply the full explanation. It creates a reason to generate alternatives. The agent may hypothesize that the identity holds only when one term is zero, that a cross-term has been omitted, or that the rule depends on a different algebraic structure. It can then test boundary cases and derive the expression using distributivity:

(a + b)^2 = (a + b)(a + b) = a^2 + ab + ba + b^2.

For real numbers, this simplifies to a^2 + 2ab + b^2. A corrected principle is that the square of a sum includes the cross-term 2ab; the simpler expression is valid only under the narrower condition ab = 0. The agent should not retain the original false rule with a vague confidence adjustment. It should kill that version, record the counterexample, and create a new version with the corrected scope.

The principle becomes more useful when connected to other principles. It depends on distributivity and the rules of multiplication. It helps explain polynomial expansion and can be used to check later algebraic transformations. Those connections form part of a mathematical worldview: a set of related claims whose conditions and dependencies are explicit. The counterexample is a powerful learning signal, the hypothesis is the object being tested, the kill stage rejects the false generalization, the principle captures the corrected relationship, and the worldview organizes it with related knowledge.

This example also illustrates an important limit. Testing a few numerical cases can kill a universal statement by producing one valid counterexample, but passing many numerical tests does not prove a universal mathematical identity. A mathematical principle requires a proof or a valid derivation when the claim is universal. LWR must use domain-appropriate standards of validation rather than treating a fixed number of successful examples as proof.

Research on mathematics learning supports the educational value of this sequence, with important qualifications. In two randomized studies, Kapur (2014) compared problem solving before instruction with instruction before problem solving. Both approaches produced strong procedural knowledge, but students who attempted the problems before instruction demonstrated greater conceptual understanding and transfer to novel problems. Research on incorrect algebra examples also suggests that explaining why an approach is wrong and identifying the underlying procedure can reduce misconceptions and support principled understanding (Barbieri & Silla, 2024). These findings do not establish that every failure is useful or that failure alone teaches. They support a narrower point: an unsuccessful attempt can prepare the learner to understand a correction more deeply when the attempt is followed by explanation and consolidation.

2.5 Assumptions are not allowed to travel as facts

The central control in LWR is explicit assumption handling. During decomposition, the system identifies direct observations, interpretations, assumptions, and unknown variables. Each item receives a source and verification status. A model-generated explanation cannot upgrade its own assumptions merely by repeating them in a more confident form.

Each hypothesis must list the assumptions on which it depends. The kill stage then handles each material assumption in one of three ways. It may be supported by independent evidence or a suitable intervention. It may be contradicted and removed from the hypothesis. Or it may remain unresolved, in which case the claim must be narrowed or made conditional on a check the receiving agent can perform. An unresolved assumption is never silently treated as true.

This is not a demand that every unknown be solved. Some environments do not permit a decisive test. LWR allows uncertainty to remain, but it changes how that uncertainty is represented. An unresolved condition may be retained as a precondition to verify before use. The unsupported explanation itself is not carried forward as established knowledge. The recipient sees the condition and its status, not an inherited story that presumes the condition has been met.

Assumption removal is therefore an operational rule, not a promise that a language model can identify every hidden premise. The system must expose assumptions it can identify, test them where possible, and prevent unresolved dependencies from being converted into factual instructions. The experimental design measures how often this rule prevents unsupported assumptions from appearing in the transferred worldview.

The worldview is the persistent product. It is a compact, versioned structure of conditional principles and their relationships. It is not a complete simulation of the environment, a transcript archive, or a claim that the system has discovered universal truth. Each principle states what is expected to hold, under which conditions, what lies outside its scope, and what evidence supports its current status. The worldview records how those principles fit together and where their relationships remain uncertain.

3. Shaman Architecture

Shaman implements LWR as an asynchronous consolidation service. Its components are separate software responsibilities with typed records and restricted write permissions. They may run as modules in one service; the architecture does not require several autonomous language-model agents to debate with one another. A simpler first implementation is easier to test, audit, and operate within a fixed budget.

3.1 Experience Observer

The Experience Observer receives task outcomes and the smallest trace slice needed to investigate them. It does not copy a full conversation or execution history by default. Each captured event receives a stable event ID, a root-incident ID, a task-family label, a scope, provenance, and a temporary pointer to the source. The root-incident ID prevents several tool logs from one run from being counted as independent evidence.

Capture can be triggered by a verified failure, an external correction, a near miss, a mismatch between predicted and observed outcome, a surprising success, a contradiction with an existing principle, or a sampled control success. No outcome label is treated as an explanation. The trigger decides which event receives attention; it does not decide what the system should learn from it.

Event selection should consider expected information value, novelty, severity, uncertainty, independence, testability, and investigation cost. Failure signals may receive higher priority when they reveal an expectation mismatch, but routine failures caused by unreliable infrastructure should not dominate the queue. A balanced sample of successes and failures is necessary to detect both false assumptions and overly narrow principles.

3.2 Evidence Decomposer

The Evidence Decomposer turns a mixed episode into separate, traceable items. At minimum, it records (a) direct observations, (b) agent-generated interpretations, (c) explicit assumptions, and (d) unknown or unobserved variables. Each item carries its source, verification method, scope, root incident, and independence status. A tool result and the agent's explanation of that result are different evidence objects.

Evidence quality is represented as a profile rather than a single confidence score. The profile records source reliability, direct observability, independence, and whether the evidence came from an observation or a controlled test. These dimensions remain separate so a strong value in one area cannot conceal a weakness in another. Embeddings may identify events that could be related, but similarity is only a retrieval hint. It cannot establish that events share a cause or authorize a promotion.

The decomposer must be allowed to mark a field as unknown. It must not fill missing conditions with a plausible narrative. When a source does not support a claim, the record says so. If the system cannot reliably distinguish an observation from an interpretation, the item remains unverified and cannot be used as direct evidence for promotion.

3.3 Hypothesis Generator

The Hypothesis Generator proposes explanations for a cluster of related evidence. It should produce competing hypotheses when more than one explanation fits the observations, and it must be allowed to return no hypothesis. A prototype may limit the number of hypotheses per cluster for cost control; the limit is an engineering choice rather than a theoretical claim.

Every hypothesis must specify its scope, proposed mechanism, assumptions, predictions, plausible alternatives, and falsifiers. The wording and scope are frozen before a test begins. For example, "retries are dangerous" is too broad to evaluate. A testable hypothesis might state that repeating a non-idempotent operation while the prior execution state is unknown can create duplicate effects. It also names boundaries: an operation known not to have executed, or an operation guaranteed to be idempotent, should not exhibit the same effect.

Hypothesis generation can be triggered by a failure, but it is not limited to failures. A surprising success may reveal a missing condition. Two successful cases with different conditions may suggest that the apparent rule is too broad. A control case may expose a confounding variable. The generator can use all such evidence while preserving which observations prompted each hypothesis.

The generator has no authority to publish a principle. It writes a versioned hypothesis for the Hypothesis Refutation Engine. If the claim changes after a test result is visible, the changed claim receives a new version and must pass the relevant gates again. It cannot inherit the earlier version's status merely because the revised wording sounds similar.

3.4 Hypothesis Refutation Engine: the kill stage

The Refutation Engine is responsible for finding tests that could make a hypothesis fail. It does not simply collect more examples that agree with the proposed explanation. Each test registers the exact hypothesis version, prediction, variable to be changed, controls, execution environment, and evaluator before the test runs. The registered prediction is immutable for that test.

The engine can use contrastive tests, interventions, boundary tests, counterexamples, prospective predictions, and cross-domain tests. A contrastive test changes a relevant condition and checks whether the predicted result changes. An intervention deliberately changes that condition while holding other factors as stable as the environment allows. A boundary test examines a nearby case where the proposed mechanism should stop applying. A counterexample can kill a universal claim when it is valid and within the claim's stated scope. A prospective test commits to a prediction before an unseen task is executed. A cross-domain test evaluates whether the mechanism transfers beyond the task family that produced it.

The kill operation has three main outcomes:

  • Killed: a valid test contradicts a necessary prediction or produces a counterexample within the stated scope. The hypothesis is rejected in that version.
  • Survived provisionally: the hypothesis passes the registered tests, but the evidence still supports only a limited scope and may not establish causality.
  • Unresolved: the tests are inconclusive, invalid, too weak, or too expensive to distinguish the hypothesis from alternatives.

A killed hypothesis is not a wasted result. Its rejection can prevent the same false explanation from being promoted again. It may also create a better hypothesis by exposing which part of the original explanation failed. A revised hypothesis receives a new version and a new test plan. The engine must not quietly rewrite the original claim after seeing the result.

The engine must keep predictive and causal claims separate. Repeated prediction success can justify a predictive regularity within a tested scope. It does not by itself prove that the proposed mechanism caused the result. Causal language requires an intervention or a suitable quasi-experimental design that can distinguish the mechanism from plausible alternatives. If the environment does not support such a test, the claim remains predictive and is labeled accordingly.

The first prototype separates three roles: Test Planner, Test Runner, and Evaluator. Their contexts and write permissions are isolated. The planner freezes the test and cannot write the result. The runner executes an allowlisted procedure and cannot edit the hypothesis. The evaluator compares the registered prediction with the raw observation and cannot rewrite that observation. Objective environment results take priority over a language-model judge wherever available. These roles may use the same underlying model initially, but their permissions and records remain separate.

Tests are prioritized by expected information value, potential impact, uncertainty, execution cost, and operational risk. The priority score schedules work; it is not a truth score and cannot promote a principle. When a test budget is exhausted, the hypothesis remains unresolved rather than receiving a lower validation standard.

3.5 Principle Manager

The Principle Manager converts surviving hypotheses into principles. It does not copy the hypothesis sentence into persistent memory and call it knowledge. It rewrites the claim so that a receiving agent can identify its scope, conditions, boundaries, evidence status, and action implications.

A principle is a versioned object, not a sentence placed in a prompt. It contains a readable statement, scope, conditions, boundaries, predictive or causal status, validation state, publication status, and an evidence certificate. A hypothesis can produce no principle if the evidence is too weak, if the conditions cannot be stated, or if the claim cannot be made useful without hiding unresolved assumptions.

Promotion must preserve the difference between what is observed and what is inferred. For example, a tool timeout is an observation; the claim that the operation definitely did not execute is an inference. A principle about safe retries may be published only if its actual preconditions are stated and can be checked by the recipient.

3.6 Worldview Composer

The Worldview Composer connects principles into a structured account. It does not treat the worldview as a bag of independent rules. Principles may support one another, depend on shared conditions, describe different levels of a mechanism, narrow one another, or conflict under apparently overlapping scopes.

The worldview is represented as a versioned graph. Its nodes are published principles. Its edges record typed relationships such as supports, depends on, narrows, contradicts, specializes, or is an exception to. Groups or domains can organize related principles without pretending that every relationship is causal. Each edge has provenance and status; a language model cannot create a confirmed dependency merely by writing two claims next to each other.

When a new principle is proposed, the composer checks it against the current worldview. If it conflicts with an existing principle, the system first checks whether their scopes or conditions differ. The conflict may be resolved by narrowing one claim, adding an explicit condition, or retaining both as context-dependent principles. If the evidence genuinely contradicts an active principle, the affected principle is suspended until revalidation. The composer creates a new worldview snapshot rather than silently overwriting history.

A worldview is not complete or permanently true. It is the current best-supported structure available to the agent within a defined domain. Its usefulness must be tested through decisions and transfer, not assumed from the elegance or internal consistency of its graph.

3.7 Handoff Manager

The Handoff Manager publishes a worldview snapshot only after each included principle has a valid certificate and the snapshot passes scope and consistency checks. It then removes the recipient's operational access to the source episodes. The source material may remain in a restricted research audit store when needed and permitted, but the receiving agent cannot retrieve it through a transcript, summary, cache, embedding index, or hidden pointer.

Publication is transactional. The previous worldview remains active if the new snapshot fails validation. Source deletion is not performed until the new snapshot is safely published and retention checks pass. The Handoff Manager records a deletion receipt describing which operational stores and retrieval paths were cleared or disabled.

4. Formal Representation

Let an active agent interact with an environment over a sequence of tasks. Shaman receives selected event slices rather than treating an entire conversation as one memory object. An event is represented as:

e_i = (x_i, a_i, yhat_i, y_i, v_i, s_i, r_i, q_i)

where x_i is the task context, a_i is the action, yhat_i is the expected outcome, y_i is the observed outcome, v_i records provenance and verification, s_i is the privacy or knowledge scope, r_i identifies the root incident, and q_i records the selection signal. The selection signal may be failure, prediction mismatch, correction, near miss, surprising success, contradiction, or control sampling.

The decomposer maps an event into four epistemic classes:

D(e_i) = (O_i, I_i, A_i, U_i)

where O_i contains observations, I_i contains interpretations, A_i contains assumptions, and U_i contains unknowns. Each item also retains provenance and status. The classes are not interchangeable. In particular, an item in I_i or A_i cannot be promoted as though it belonged to O_i.

A hypothesis is represented as:

h_j = (C_j, M_j, P_j, F_j, A_j, X_j, E_j, V_j)

where C_j defines scope and conditions, M_j is the proposed mechanism, P_j contains registered predictions, F_j contains falsifiers, A_j lists material assumptions and their statuses, X_j contains alternative explanations, E_j points to the evidence items used to construct the hypothesis, and V_j is its immutable version identifier.

A test t_k registers the hypothesis ID and version, prediction, changed variable, controls, environment, executor, and evaluator before execution. Its result is recorded independently from the raw observation. A test written or materially changed after its result is visible cannot be counted as prospective evidence for that version.

A principle is represented as:

p_m = (S_m, C_m, B_m, K_m, Z_m, Cert_m)

where S_m is the conditional statement, C_m is the scope and required conditions, B_m is the boundary set, K_m is the predictive or causal status, Z_m is the validation and publication state, and Cert_m is the evidence certificate.

The worldview at version t is represented as:

W_t = (P_t, R_t, G_t, C_t, H_t)

where P_t is the set of published principles, R_t is the set of typed relationships among principles, G_t organizes principles into domain or mechanism groups, C_t records known conflicts and unresolved conditions, and H_t records snapshot history and provenance. A relationship is itself evidence-bearing. For example, a dependency edge must state why one principle depends on another and which evidence supports that connection.

The consolidation process accepts selected events E, an approved test environment M, and a resource budget B. It produces candidate principles P, a worldview W, and an evidence ledger L:

LWR(E, M, B) -> (P, W, L).

The receiving agent receives a published version of W and the compact certificates required to interpret its principles. It does not receive E, a searchable index of E, or an alternate pointer that recreates access to the source episodes. In the primary experiment, the foundation model weights remain fixed so transfer from the worldview can be measured separately from parameter updates.

The objective is not to convert every event into a principle. A cluster may produce no hypothesis. A hypothesis may be killed. A surviving hypothesis may remain unresolved. A principle may be local to one task family. A new principle may add nothing useful to the current worldview. An empty worldview update is a valid result. Shaman is allowed to conclude that the available experience does not justify anything new being carried forward.

5. How Knowledge Earns Its Place

A hypothesis does not become a principle because it is repeated, internally coherent, or written convincingly. It must pass explicit gates. These gates describe what the evidence permits the receiving agent to do with a claim; they do not establish universal truth.

StateMinimum gateRecipient use
SignalAn event contains a verified discrepancy, correction, surprising outcome, or control observation worth investigating.No retrieval.
HypothesisA scoped explanation exists. Observations, interpretations, assumptions, unknowns, and alternatives are separated.No retrieval.

The initial prototype may use three independent root incidents for evidence support, five registered held-out predictions with at least four matching the pre-registered outcome for predictive status, and successful prediction in two task families for transfer status. These thresholds are configurable engineering defaults, not statistical guarantees or universal rules. They are not suitable as proof standards for mathematical theorems. A proper study must report them and test sensitivity to alternative thresholds and domain-specific validation requirements.

The assumption gate is independent of the prediction count. A hypothesis cannot pass merely because its predictions happen to match when a material assumption remains hidden. For every assumption, the system records whether it was tested, contradicted, supported, or left unresolved. If unresolved, it must either narrow the claim so the assumption is no longer required or express the assumption as a precondition that the recipient must verify before use. If neither is possible, the hypothesis is not promoted.

Evidence quality remains multidimensional. The certificate records source reliability, observability, incident independence, controlled-test status, and evaluator type separately. Multiple records from one incident do not count as multiple independent incidents. A claim may be predictively supported without being causally established; the certificate must preserve this distinction.

A principle's publication does not make it operational by default. The receiving agent must check its conditions and apply it only within its tested scope. Retrieval first filters for published status, scope match, and conditions known to hold. It then ranks eligible principles by relevance, prospective reliability, independent evidence, boundary coverage, and measured decision value. If a required condition is unknown, the principle is returned only as a conditional possibility or withheld, depending on the risk policy. It must never be returned as an unconditional instruction.

6. Worldview Construction and Revision

The worldview is the layer that turns principles into transferable understanding. Without it, a system can accumulate a collection of locally useful rules while remaining unable to represent how those rules relate. A principle may be true only because another condition holds. Two principles may appear inconsistent because they apply to different operating regimes. A general principle may be narrowed by a later discovery. The worldview makes these relationships explicit.

6.1 A principle is not yet a worldview

A principle answers a scoped question: what relationship has survived testing, and under which conditions? A worldview addresses a larger set of questions: which principles depend on one another, which mechanisms connect them, where do they conflict, and what remains unknown? The worldview is therefore not created by simply concatenating principle statements.

For example, the retry principle in Appendix A may connect to principles about idempotency, state verification, distributed operations, and duplicate side effects. The worldview can express that safe retry behavior depends on knowledge of execution state or on an operation's idempotency property. A receiving agent can then reason through the relevant conditions instead of retrieving one isolated instruction.

6.2 Relationship types

The initial implementation uses a small set of typed relationships:

RelationshipMeaningRequired safeguard
SupportsEvidence for one principle also supports another.The shared evidence and logical connection must be recorded.
Depends onA principle requires another condition or mechanism.The dependency must be checked before use.
NarrowsA newer principle restricts the scope of an older one.The older version remains auditable but may be suspended or superseded.
ContradictsTwo principles make incompatible claims under overlapping conditions.Both are withheld from conflicting use until scope or evidence is reviewed.
SpecializesA principle describes a narrower case of a broader principle.The broader principle cannot be applied outside its own tested scope.
Exception toA principle identifies a verified case where a broader rule does not apply.The exception conditions must be explicit and testable.

The composer should not force every pair of principles into a relationship. An unlinked pair is preferable to an invented connection. If the system cannot explain why two principles are related, it leaves them separate.

6.3 Contradiction and revision

A serious contradiction triggers revalidation, not automatic deletion. The composer checks whether the principles apply to different conditions, whether the new evidence is independent, whether a tool or model change altered the environment, and whether either claim depended on an unsupported assumption. If the conflict is genuine, the affected principle is suspended or narrowed. The revised worldview receives a new snapshot.

A worldview can be incomplete and still useful. It must expose unresolved conditions and conflicting principles rather than hide them behind a single confidence score. A new agent should be able to see both what the system currently supports and where its account remains uncertain.

6.4 The worldview is provisional

The term worldview does not imply a complete or objective representation of reality. It refers to the best-supported structure the system has built from a limited set of experiences, tests, and domain constraints. It is versioned, scoped, and revisable. Its quality must be measured by transfer, calibration, boundary recognition, and decision outcomes, not by size or apparent coherence.

7. Forgetting and the Handoff

Forgetting in LWR is a defined access and retention operation. After a worldview snapshot is published and verified, the source episodes are removed from the receiving agent's operational memory. The recipient has no retrieval index, pointer, cache, or permission path that exposes the original records. A new task begins with the same base model and tools, but with the published worldview instead of the source episodes.

Shaman uses three storage zones. The Experience Buffer holds selected event slices while they are under investigation. The Evidence Ledger stores provenance, decomposed evidence, assumption status, hypothesis versions, registered predictions, test results, and promotion history. The Worldview Store holds published principles and their relationships. The ledger must not become a second transcript archive. After a successful publication, raw event text and temporary source pointers are deleted from the operational learning store. A compact, non-reconstructive certificate remains to explain why a principle has its current status.

A research benchmark may retain a sealed audit record outside the recipient's retrieval boundary when needed to reproduce the experiment and permitted by the applicable data policy. That record is not available to the receiving agent and is not part of its inherited context. Deletion must be verified across stores that could expose source episodes, including indexes and caches. Merely clearing the prompt or starting a new conversation is not sufficient.

The primary experiment keeps foundation-model weights fixed. Shaman does not fine-tune the source or receiving agent on the source episodes or on the worldview. This restriction isolates the effect of the handoff. If a model has already absorbed the source experiences through weight updates, deleting an external file does not demonstrate learning without episodic retention.

The handoff is successful only if a new agent can use the retained worldview to make a better decision on a new task while the source episodes remain unavailable. Exact recall of the old episode is not the goal. The goal is transfer of supported relationships, including their conditions and limits. A receiver that repeats a principle but fails to check its conditions has not demonstrated successful transfer. A receiver that follows one principle but ignores a relevant dependency or contradiction has not demonstrated successful worldview use.

8. Related Work

8.1 Learning from errors and failure in mathematics

Research on productive failure provides a useful foundation for treating an unsuccessful attempt as a learning opportunity rather than as an event to erase. Kapur's study of productive failure found that students who worked on complex problems before receiving instruction initially produced weaker solutions, yet later showed stronger near- and far-transfer performance than students who began with well-structured problems (Kapur, 2008). In a more directly mathematical study, Kapur (2014) reported two randomized studies in which problem solving before instruction led to greater conceptual understanding and transfer to novel problems, even though procedural knowledge was high in both conditions. Kapur's later account describes the design as a generation and exploration phase followed by consolidation and knowledge assembly (Kapur, 2015). That sequence is relevant to LWR: a failed attempt can expose the learner's current assumptions, but the later consolidation phase is what helps turn the attempt into understanding.

Research on incorrect worked examples in algebra offers a complementary result. Barbieri and Silla (2024) analyzed student explanations and problem-solving errors from a classroom experiment. Their analyses identified reductions in targeted misconceptions and more principled explanations of procedures as mechanisms that helped explain learning benefits. The implication is not that an incorrect example automatically teaches. The learner benefits when the error is used to inspect the reasoning and the structure of the correct procedure.

Metcalfe's review of learning from errors concludes that errorful learning followed by corrective feedback can be beneficial, and that analyzing the reasoning that led to the error is important (Metcalfe, 2017). Ohlsson's theory explains performance errors as conflicts between what a learner expects and what is observed, with correction requiring the faulty knowledge to be specialized so that it is activated only in appropriate conditions (Ohlsson, 1996). This account is closely related to LWR's emphasis on scope and boundaries: correcting an overgeneral rule often means narrowing where it applies, not simply replacing one sentence with another.

Recent syntheses also caution against treating error exposure as a universal intervention. Dieterich, Rumann, and Rodemer's systematic review of 40 studies found that erroneous examples can improve learning, but effects depend on how errors are explained or highlighted, the prompts used, learners' prior knowledge, and cognitive demands (Dieterich et al., 2025). Alemdag, Eichelmann, and Narciss (2025) likewise synthesize experimental research on learning from others' erroneous examples. These findings support a qualified claim: errors can create a productive opportunity for learning when they are examined and corrected, but unprocessed error exposure is not enough.

This literature is especially relevant to the LWR sequence because it distinguishes failure from learning. An unsuccessful attempt can help generate hypotheses and reveal misconceptions. Explanation, testing, correction, and consolidation determine whether that information becomes transferable understanding. LWR applies the same distinction to agents and adds explicit controls over what is allowed to persist and what the receiving agent can inherit.

8.2 Agents that learn from their own trajectories

Prior work has established that agents can extract and reuse knowledge from interaction histories without necessarily updating model weights. Reflexion stores verbal feedback in episodic memory for later attempts (Shinn et al., 2023). ExpeL extracts natural-language insights from training tasks and recalls both insights and past experiences during inference (Zhao et al., 2024). LEAP intentionally induces mistakes, derives task-specific principles from them, and uses those principles on unseen questions, including mathematical reasoning benchmarks (Zhang et al., 2024). These systems establish the value of experience-based adaptation and, in LEAP's case, show that mistakes can be a direct source of reusable principles.

Recent work makes the overlap with LWR more direct. Mistake Notebook Learning clusters failures into reusable notes and updates external memory when batch performance improves; its evaluations include mathematical reasoning, Text-to-SQL, and interactive agent benchmarks (Su et al., 2026). SkillForge pairs successful and failed attempts on the same task to identify procedural choices associated with different outcomes, then filters and retrieves guidance on demand (Wan et al., 2026). EvolveR distills both successful and failed trajectories into strategic principles, scores their utility, and uses the resulting experience base during later interaction and policy evolution (Wu et al., 2026).

LWR does not claim that extracting principles from experience, learning from mistakes, or pairing successful and failed trajectories is new. Its additional requirements are a defined hypothesis lifecycle, an explicit kill stage, assumption gating, a structured principle-to-worldview composition layer, and a source-free transfer test. The recipient is evaluated after the source episodes are unavailable, not merely on whether it can reuse them through retrieval.

Work on agent memory governance also treats consolidation as a decision that should be evaluated rather than assumed to be beneficial. Annapureddy and Thamatani (2026) examine how long-horizon agents decide what to retain, abstract, or forget, and propose ways to assess and govern those decisions. Their work is a recent preprint and should be treated as emerging research. LWR shares the concern with governed consolidation while focusing on the path from discrepancy to hypothesis, from hypothesis to tested principle, and from principles to a worldview that can be transferred without the source episodes.

8.3 The specific distinction

The novelty claim is deliberately narrow. LWR is not a claim to have invented reflection, productive failure, counterexample-based reasoning, principle extraction, or memory compression. It proposes an explicit boundary between temporary experience and inherited understanding. Failures and other informative discrepancies can generate hypotheses; hypotheses must face attempts to kill them; surviving claims become conditional principles; principles are connected into a worldview; and a new agent is evaluated without access to the experiences that produced it. The central contribution is the combined governance and evaluation of this full lifecycle.

9. Experimental Design

The evaluation should separate the components of LWR rather than compare a complete system with an unrelated baseline. The same foundation model, tool set, task distribution, context budget, and compute budget should be used wherever possible. Training and evaluation should use separate task instances and held-out task families. The primary evaluation should measure actual decisions, not whether the recipient can restate a stored principle.

9.1 Research questions

RQ1. Does using verified failures and prediction mismatches as high-information triggers produce better hypotheses than selecting only successful outcomes or sampling uniformly?

RQ2. Do the explicit hypothesis lifecycle and kill stage reduce false promotion compared with reflection or critique alone?

RQ3. Does assumption decomposition reduce the transfer of unsupported assumptions into principles and the worldview?

RQ4. Can a new agent use the resulting worldview to improve decisions on unfamiliar tasks after source episodes are removed?

RQ5. Does connecting principles into a worldview improve transfer and boundary recognition compared with giving the recipient the same principles as an unstructured list?

9.2 Study 1: Hypothesis generation and the kill stage

The first study isolates the epistemic controls. The same event pool and candidate generator are used across conditions, while the consolidation procedure changes. One condition uses ordinary reflection or summary-based extraction. A second labels observations, interpretations, assumptions, and unknowns but does not run independent tests. A third uses the full LWR process, including competing hypotheses, an assumption register, pre-registered predictions, and an explicit kill stage. Model-call and token budgets are matched.

The evaluator measures whether unsupported or contradicted assumptions appear in the final inherited principles. It also checks whether promoted principles predict held-out cases and whether their stated conditions match the evidence. Killed hypotheses are counted and audited. A system that kills every hypothesis is not successful; it must also preserve claims that survive appropriate tests. This study tests whether the kill stage improves selectivity without producing excessive false rejection.

9.3 Study 2: Cross-agent transfer after removal

The transfer study compares recipients that differ only in the information made available to them. The source agent produces the same experience pool for each condition. A separate recipient receives one of the following:

ConditionRecipient receives
BaseNo additional information from the source agent.
EpisodicOriginal source episodes through ordinary retrieval.
SummaryConventional summaries of the source episodes.
Unvalidated hypothesesCandidate explanations without independent refutation or assumption gating.
Isolated principlesValidated principles as a flat list, without worldview relationships.
LWR worldviewValidated principles and their worldview relationships, with compact certificates; source episodes are unavailable.

All recipients use the same foundation model and tools. Only the memory condition changes. The receiving agent starts from a clean task context and is not given the source agent's full conversation. In the LWR condition, deletion is checked across indexes, caches, source pointers, and other retrieval routes. The recipient records which principle it used, which preconditions it checked, what relationships it followed, and what it predicted before acting.

An ablation withholds the relevant principle from the recipient on a subset of tasks. This checks whether the worldview actually changed the decision. Another ablation removes the evidence status or boundary information to measure whether the recipient becomes more likely to apply a principle outside its scope. A further ablation keeps the individual principles but removes their relationships, testing whether worldview composition contributes beyond principle extraction alone.

9.4 Study 3: Event-selection policy

A secondary study compares the event-selection signals that determine which experiences receive the limited investigation budget. The experience pool includes noisy outcomes: some failures arise from environmental causes, and some successful outcomes may depend on luck or unobserved conditions. The selection policies differ only in which events are investigated.

PolicySelection rule
Success-onlyInvestigate verified successful outcomes.
Failure-onlyInvestigate verified failed outcomes.
UniformSample events without using outcome labels.
Mismatch-priorityPrioritize verified prediction mismatches, external corrections, near misses, and surprising outcomes; include success and failure controls.
Balanced signal portfolioAllocate a fixed share of the budget to failures, surprising successes, boundary cases, and randomly sampled controls.

Each policy receives the same event and processing budget. Candidate principles are evaluated on a separate held-out set. The study does not reward a selector for finding more failures. It tests which trigger produces the most useful hypotheses and the most reliable principles per unit of investigation cost. The balanced signal portfolio is the main LWR candidate; failure-only is a comparison condition, not the default architecture.

9.5 Measures and falsifiable outcomes

MeasureWhat it tests
Decision qualityWhether the recipient makes better choices on held-out tasks.
Hypothesis yieldThe number of useful, testable hypotheses generated per investigated event.
Kill precisionThe fraction of killed hypotheses that were genuinely contradicted under valid tests.
False kill rateThe fraction of rejected hypotheses that could have survived under a valid test or correctly stated scope.
Assumption carryover rateFraction of unsupported, contradicted, or untested assumptions that appear as factual claims in the inherited principles or worldview.
False promotion rateFraction of promoted principles whose registered predictions fail under their stated conditions.
Boundary accuracyWhether the recipient recognizes cases where a principle should not apply.
Scope violation rateHow often a recipient uses a principle when required conditions are false or unknown.
Worldview consistencyWhether connected principles have unresolved contradictions or unsupported dependency links.
Transfer accuracyWhether predictions remain accurate in held-out task families.
Episodic dependenceHow much exact recall or access to source episodes is needed to make the decision.
Resource costConsolidation tokens, model calls, test execution cost, latency, and retained context size.

The main prediction is that the LWR worldview handoff will reduce the carryover of unsupported assumptions while preserving or improving decision quality on held-out tasks compared with ordinary summaries and unvalidated hypotheses. A second prediction is that the kill stage will reduce false promotion relative to reflection or critique alone under matched budgets, without an unacceptable increase in false kills. A third prediction is that a recipient given the validated worldview will transfer more decision-relevant knowledge than a recipient given only compressed episodes or an unstructured list of principles, while showing lower exact recall of the source episodes.

The event-selection study has a more limited prediction: mismatch-priority and balanced-signal policies may produce more useful hypotheses per unit cost than success-only, failure-only, or uniform sampling. The result is empirical, not assumed. If failure-only sampling performs best in a particular domain, that result should be reported rather than explained away. If success signals are equally or more useful in some domains, LWR should allow the selector to adapt.

The proposal is weakened if explicit assumption separation does not reduce unsupported assumption carryover, if the kill stage does not improve the reliability of promoted claims, or if a source-free recipient cannot use the worldview on unfamiliar tasks. It is also weakened if apparent gains disappear under changed task wording, arise only from additional computation, or are offset by false rejection or negative transfer. These outcomes should be reported, including killed hypotheses, failed tests, unresolved assumptions, and negative-transfer cases, rather than presenting only the principles that survived.

10. Implementation Specification

Shaman should be implemented as a service with a queue, persistent records, and a retrieval interface. The components described above may run in one process or as workers behind a queue. The active agent submits selected events to the observer. Background workers perform decomposition, hypothesis generation, refutation, principle formation, worldview composition, and publication. The retrieval service reads only a published worldview snapshot.

A relational database is the canonical store. PostgreSQL is suitable for a multi-user deployment; SQLite is sufficient for a local prototype. Model-specific payloads may be stored as JSON, but identity, version, scope, status, source lineage, and relationships should be queryable without asking a language model to interpret a file. Embeddings may help find candidate clusters, but cannot act as the source of truth or promote a claim.

Every record that can affect retrieval must point to a particular principle version and an immutable evidence certificate. Hypotheses and principles are versioned; revisions never overwrite prior versions. Each event has a root-incident ID for independence checks. A unique idempotency key prevents retried consolidation jobs from creating duplicate records.

Publication is transactional. The retrieval service cannot see a partial worldview update. A new snapshot becomes visible only after all promoted principles have certificates and relationship checks have passed. Source deletion occurs only after publication and retention checks succeed. If publication fails, the previous snapshot remains active and source records remain available to complete or roll back the run. The deletion operation returns a receipt listing the operational stores that were cleared or made inaccessible.

Retrieval has two stages. The first removes hypotheses, unpublished principles, suspended principles, scope mismatches, and claims whose required conditions are known to be false. The second ranks eligible principles and relevant worldview relationships for the current task. The default result should contain no more than three principles plus only the relationships needed to interpret them. Each item includes its statement, scope, known conditions, unknown conditions, boundaries, validation status, and evidence certificate ID. If no principle qualifies, retrieval returns an empty result rather than filling the context with weak matches.

When the active agent uses a principle to make a prediction, it passes a prediction ID to the feedback interface. The observed result is linked to that prediction. This feedback can trigger revalidation, but the active agent's own explanation of the result is not counted as an independent verifier. Model, prompt, tool, and environment versions are recorded so changes in the operating context can be distinguished from changes in evidence.

11. Limitations

LWR still relies on language models to identify assumptions and propose hypotheses. A model may fail to notice an implicit premise, and a second model may share the same blind spot. Separate contexts and permissions reduce some forms of contamination but do not guarantee independent judgment. Objective tests, executable checks, controlled environments, mathematical proof checkers, and human review should be used where appropriate.

A failure is not automatically informative. It may result from environmental noise, unavailable tools, an incorrect benchmark, ambiguous instructions, or a factor unrelated to the agent's reasoning. A successful outcome can also be misleading if it depends on luck or an unobserved intervention. LWR must evaluate the quality and independence of evidence rather than assuming that negative outcomes are always more useful than positive ones.

Some hypotheses cannot be decisively tested. In observational settings, several mechanisms may explain the same outcome. LWR must preserve this uncertainty and avoid causal language when the evidence supports only a predictive relationship. A principle may be useful within a narrow scope without being a general law. Transfer gates reduce the risk of overgeneralization but cannot eliminate it.

The kill stage has a real cost. Some hypotheses are cheap to test; others require simulators, sandboxed tools, formal verification, or human evaluation. The system needs a budget and a policy for prioritizing tests according to expected information value, uncertainty, cost, and operational risk. If testing costs more than the value of the knowledge produced, LWR may not be practical for that task family. An exhausted budget must leave a hypothesis unresolved rather than lower the validation standard.

A worldview can become internally tidy while remaining wrong. A graph of mutually supporting principles may reflect a shared mistaken assumption. This is why evidence certificates must remain attached to individual principles and relationships, and why the entire worldview must be tested through held-out decisions. Structural coherence is useful but is not a substitute for evidence.

A receiving agent may ignore, misread, or overapply a compact principle. The presence of a principle in the prompt is not evidence that it improved behavior. Evaluation must measure decisions, precondition checks, boundary recognition, and controlled removal effects. The framework should not replace all forms of memory. Exact facts, current state, and active commitments may still require ordinary retrieval. LWR concerns the conversion of experience into reusable understanding, not the storage of every fact an agent needs to operate.

Finally, deletion must be interpreted within the system's retention policy. A research audit or external record may be required for reproducibility or compliance, but it must remain outside the receiving agent's retrieval boundary. A system that claims to forget while retaining hidden access to the source episode has not satisfied the LWR handoff condition.

12. Conclusion

An agent can retain an experience without learning from it. It can also inherit an experience's assumptions without knowing that they were assumptions. Summarizing the record does not resolve either problem. The central question is not how much history a system can preserve, but what it has established well enough to pass on.

Learning Without Retention separates those operations. It treats experiences as temporary evidence, gives special attention to failures and other informative discrepancies, separates observations from interpretations and assumptions, and generates competing hypotheses that must face attempts to kill them. Hypotheses that survive the relevant tests may become conditional principles. Those principles are connected into a versioned worldview that records their relationships, dependencies, conflicts, and limits. Successful outcomes remain part of the evidence because they help establish where a principle works and where its boundaries lie.

The receiving agent gets the supported worldview and the evidence status needed to use it responsibly, while source episodes and unsupported assumptions are removed from its operational access. The decisive evaluation is whether a new agent can make better decisions on unfamiliar tasks without access to the episodes that produced the worldview. If it cannot, the system has compressed history without demonstrating transferable learning. If it can, and if unsupported assumptions are measurably prevented from crossing the handoff, LWR will provide evidence for a different way to build persistent agents: preserve what has survived testing, connect it into an evolving worldview, and do not make the next agent inherit the whole story.

Unique constraints on hypothesis and principle ID-version pairs prevent revisions from overwriting history. The root incident ID is indexed for evidence-independence checks. Retrieval filters by publication status, scope, and conditions before ranking. Validation state, publication status, and causal status are separate fields: transfer-tested claims may be published within their tested scope, while operational status requires a further controlled-use comparison against a matched baseline. A principle can be predictively tested without being causally established.

Appendix C. Runtime Interface

Capture and retrieval may be synchronous. Consolidation, testing, publication, and revalidation are asynchronous because they may require execution in a sandbox. Each asynchronous operation returns a run ID that can be inspected.

A retrieval response contains the worldview version, applicable principles, relevant relationships, unknown conditions, current contradictions, unresolved questions, and token count. The retrieval service returns no more than three principles by default. When a required condition is unknown, the response preserves that uncertainty rather than silently treating it as true. An empty result is valid.

{
  "worldview_version": "wv_0012",
  "applicable_principles": [],
  "relevant_relationships": [],
  "unknown_conditions": [],
  "relevant_contradictions": [],
  "unresolved_questions": [],
  "token_count": 0
}

Publication is atomic. The new worldview becomes visible only after every included principle has a certificate and all relationship and scope checks have passed. Deletion runs only after publication and retention checks succeed. If publication fails, the previous snapshot remains active and the source records remain available to finish or roll back the run. The receiving agent has no credentials or index route that can access the source events.

Appendix D. Acceptance Tests and Reporting Requirements

The implementation is ready for the transfer experiment only when the following conditions can be demonstrated:

  1. Repeated log entries from one root incident do not count as independent evidence.
  2. Observed outcomes are stored separately from agent interpretations.
  3. Assumptions and unknowns are explicitly represented and cannot be silently promoted as facts.
  4. Failures, surprising successes, and routine controls can all enter the evidence-selection process.
  5. A hypothesis cannot start a test without a frozen version and registered prediction.
  6. The test runner cannot edit the hypothesis or write the evaluator verdict.
  7. A valid counterexample can kill a universal claim within its stated scope.
  8. An invalid test contributes neither support nor contradiction.
  9. A revised hypothesis receives a new version and does not inherit promotion automatically.
  10. A principle outside its scope, suspended, or contradicted under current conditions is not returned as applicable.
  11. A worldview relationship cannot be treated as confirmed without provenance and a validation status.
  12. A retrieval result can be empty and does not fill context with weak matches.
  13. A failed worldview publication leaves the previous snapshot active and does not delete source events.
  14. A successful publication is followed by removal of operational source events and all recipient access paths.
  15. Every retrieved principle can be traced to its versioned evidence certificate.
  16. Every worldview relationship can be traced to its own provenance and validation status.

A result should not be reported as evidence for LWR unless the study states the foundation model and version, task environments, training and held-out split, event-selection policy, assumption-decomposition procedure, hypothesis-generation procedure, model-call and context budgets, test-generation procedure, evaluator type, promotion thresholds, worldview-composition rules, retrieval budget, and source-deletion verification. The report should include rejected hypotheses, failed tests, unresolved assumptions, false kills, negative-transfer cases, and the rate of empty worldview updates where data policy permits. Reporting only the principles that survived would conceal how selective the process was.

The transfer experiment must verify that the recipient cannot retrieve a source episode through another index, cache, prompt, or storage path. A clean context is not enough if a hidden summary contains the same information. The audit records which stores were made inaccessible and which worldview version the recipient received.
